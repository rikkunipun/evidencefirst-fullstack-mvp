# Belief discovery protocol v3

## Research hypothesis

People do not reliably retrieve an abstract belief on request. A better route is:

1. learn the person's current role and decisions;
2. recall one concrete event;
3. separate the expected outcome from social pressure and practical barriers;
4. extract a claim only when the expected outcome caused the decision;
5. park preferences, values, constraints and behavior gaps.

## Stage 0: 60-second context scan

Ask:

1. **What occupies most of your week right now: school, college, work, household, business, job search, retirement or something else?**
2. **What is one goal or responsibility taking most of your attention this month?**
3. **Which area should we discuss: study/work, money/purchases, health/routines, technology, or family/household decisions?**

Use the answer to select examples. Do not assume that age alone defines the person's concerns.

## Stage 1: role-specific event prompts

### School-age participant

Use only if the research design permits minors and the required guardian consent and child assent are in place.

- Tell me about one choice you made at school or home last week.
- Was there something you did not try because you thought it would go badly?
- Did a friend, parent, teacher or video tell you something that changed what you did?
- What did you think would happen?

Use short sentences, familiar words and one question at a time. Allow a long pause before rephrasing.

### College student or job seeker

- Which application, exam, course or skill did you choose or skip recently?
- What did you expect would happen if you chose the other path?
- Did you avoid any opportunity because you thought it would not be useful or safe?
- What did a senior, friend, teacher, reel or online post influence you to do?

### Working adult

- Which work, purchase, subscription, commute, financial or health decision did you make recently?
- What alternative did you reject, and what did you expect from it?
- What advice from a colleague, professional, family member or online source changed your decision?
- What measurable time or money changed because of that decision?

### Parent, household decision-maker or older adult

- What advice do you regularly give your family because you believe it prevents a problem?
- Which household, health, insurance, savings or technology choice did you make recently?
- What do you avoid because you believe it may be unsafe, wasteful or unreliable?
- Tell me about the most recent time that belief changed what you or your family did.

## Stage 2: reconstruct one incident

1. **Tell me what happened, starting just before you decided.**
2. **What options did you have?**
3. **Which option did you choose?**
4. **What did you expect would happen with each option?**
5. **Where did each expectation come from?**

Ask for facts before asking “why.” A later explanation may be useful, but it should not replace the event record.

## Stage 3: belief, norm or barrier

Ask these separately:

### Outcome belief

> Did you reject the alternative because you thought it would produce a bad or useless result? What result?

### Social expectation

> Did another person's expectations or possible reaction affect the decision? Whose reaction?

### Control barrier

> Even if you believed the alternative would work, what practical thing would still make it difficult: time, money, skill, habit, access or confidence?

Classification:

- If the expected outcome caused the decision, a belief candidate exists.
- If social approval caused it, record a normative or identity-sensitive case; do not disguise it as a factual error.
- If time, money, habit, access or skill caused it, record a control barrier or behavior gap.
- More than one may be present. Record them separately.

## Stage 4: confirm a candidate

> Please complete this sentence in your own words: “I chose ___ instead of ___ because I expected ___.”

Then reflect it:

> I heard: “If I ___, then ___; therefore I ___.” Is that exact? What should I change?

Do not continue until the participant confirms it.

## Stage 5: baseline and crux check

Record confidence in the participant-confirmed belief **before** testing reasons:

> On a scale from 0 to 10, how confident are you in that exact statement right now?

Then identify one provisionally load-bearing reason:

1. **What is the main reason you are at that number?**
2. Reflect it back and ask: **Is that your main reason, or is something else more important?**
3. Ask neutrally: **Suppose that reason turned out not to be true. What would your confidence be then, from 0 to 10?**
4. If the number does not change, ask once: **What other reason is doing more of the work?** Then repeat the stability check.
5. Stop after two candidate reasons. Do not descend into personal or emotionally charged material.

Treat a reason as a **provisional crux** when the participant identifies it as important, removing it produces a stated confidence decrease, and credible evidence or a feasible personal test can address it. Record the original score, hypothetical score, difference, exact reason wording and confirmation. Never imply that confidence should decrease; a zero difference is valid data.

Before retrieving evidence, classify the confirmed reason as one of four types:

- current observable claim;
- near-term personal prediction that can be tested;
- distant or open-ended forecast;
- value, identity or emotional commitment.

Current claims may proceed to audited sources. Near-term predictions may proceed to a feasible personal test. Distant forecasts must be narrowed or parked because evidence about the present does not settle an indefinite future. Values and identity commitments are preserved and parked from factual persuasion.

Ask for the exact belief score once more after the crux conversation and before showing evidence. Record it as `pre_evidence_score`; do not infer it from the baseline or later response. This separates movement caused by questioning from movement caused by evidence.

If the reason is primarily identity, belonging, emotion, moral value or another non-checkable commitment, record it and park it from factual persuasion. Do not use a clinical “downward arrow” process to search for deeper core beliefs.

The crux may turn out to be true. In that case, preserve or qualify the original belief rather than forcing a correction.

## Stage 6: eligibility gates

A persuasion candidate must pass all six gates:

1. **Current:** the participant still holds the belief.
2. **Specific:** it names an action, expected outcome and relevant scope or time.
3. **Causal:** the belief materially caused the decision.
4. **Consequential:** an actual money, time or health cost has already occurred.
5. **Checkable:** credible evidence or a feasible personal test can address it.
6. **Safe and in scope:** it is not political/identity persuasion, and health-sensitive cases have appropriate boundaries.

A scoring total cannot replace a failed gate.

## Stage 7: symmetric evidence questions

1. **What would make this belief more likely to be true?**
2. **What would make it less likely to be true?**
3. **Would a reliable source be enough, or would you need personal experience?**
4. **How confident are you from 0 to 10?**

Avoid asking only “What evidence would change your mind?” It can imply that the interviewer has already decided the belief is wrong.

## Method status

The crux loop is a product hypothesis informed by Street Epistemology's reason-weighting and stability check and by the crux concept used in Double Crux. These are elicitation frameworks, not proof that a reason is causal or that the intervention will work. EvidenceFirst must measure whether this loop improves reason identification in its own sessions.

The 2024 AI-dialogue study by Costello, Pennycook and Rand is relevant because it elicited participants' own beliefs and supporting evidence before a tailored dialogue. An editorial expression of concern was published in 2026. Do not use its reported effect size as EvidenceFirst's performance target; preserve it only as design precedent and report the concern.

## Five-minute stop rule

Stop the branch if any of these remain missing:

- a specific recent event;
- an alternative;
- an expected outcome;
- confirmed wording;
- a belief-to-decision link.

Code the outcome as preference, value, social norm, control barrier, behavior gap, resolved belief or no belief.

## Next-five-interview test

Measure:

- time to classify the first branch;
- number of branches parked within five minutes;
- number with participant-confirmed belief wording;
- number with an actual verified consequence;
- corrections made by participants to the interviewer’s restatement;
- which lens identified the real bottleneck: outcome, social expectation or control.

The goal is not to increase the percentage of interviews that produce candidates. The goal is to classify cases accurately and find a small number that pass every gate.
